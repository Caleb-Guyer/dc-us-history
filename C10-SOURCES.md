# Chapter 10 coverage and sources

This game and its question bank follow the supplied edited Chapter 10, **Jacksonian Democracy, 1820–1840**. Six short episodes connect electoral politics, party organization, tariffs, banking, Native sovereignty and the 1840 campaign. The quiz provides more detailed review than the campaign alone. Completion is practice, not a prediction of a quiz grade.

Eliza Reed, her coworkers and their errands are fictional. Dialogue is original dramatization, not historical quotations. Collecting a return does not change an election; routing cargo does not win an invented tariff war; sorting money does not prevent the Panic of 1837. The court-record episode is an investigation of legal resistance and its limits. It does not let the player reverse forced removal.

## Elections and participation

Jackson led the reported popular vote and electoral count in 1824 but lacked the required electoral majority. Of 261 electoral votes, Jackson had 99, Adams 84, Crawford 41 and Clay 37; a majority required 131. The House, voting by state delegation under the Twelfth Amendment, chose Adams on February 9, 1825. Clay subsequently became secretary of state. Jacksonians alleged a corrupt bargain; a secret corrupt agreement was not proved. See the [National Archives electoral results](https://www.archives.gov/electoral-college/1824), its [1824 tally lesson](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/electoral-tally), and its [account of the House decision](https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2020/10/22/the-1824-presidential-election-and-the-corrupt-bargain/).

The expansion of White male suffrage did not create equal political participation for everyone. Women, enslaved people, most free Black people and Native peoples faced exclusions or dispossession. State rules changed at different times; the chapter's “universal manhood suffrage” is a historically bounded term, not universal adult voting rights.

## Tariffs, federal authority and banking

Clay's American System combined protective tariffs, a national bank and federal support for roads, canals and other internal improvements. The Senate's [introduction to Clay's American System speech](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/common/generic/Speeches_ClayAmericanSystem.htm) explains those linked policies.

Calhoun defended nullification as a claimed state power to void federal law. Jackson rejected that claim and defended the Union. The Force Bill authorized federal enforcement, while the Compromise Tariff of 1833 lowered rates and defused the immediate confrontation. South Carolina's response did not establish a lawful general power to cancel federal laws. See the Senate's [Calhoun biography](https://www.senate.gov/art-artifacts/fine-art/sculpture/22_00007_000.htm) and [Finance Committee history](https://www.finance.senate.gov/download/history-of-the-committee-on-finance-united-states-s-doc-97-5%26download%3D1).

The Second Bank received a federal charter in 1816. Jackson vetoed early recharter in 1832; federal deposits shifted toward state banks in 1833. Biddle restricted credit. The veto did not instantly close the Bank: its federal charter expired in 1836. See [Federal Reserve History](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/second-bank-of-the-us) and the Senate's [account of Jackson's censure](https://www.senate.gov/about/parties-leadership/censure-president-jackson.htm).

Specie means gold or silver. The 1836 Specie Circular concerned federal public-land purchases, not every purchase in the economy. The Panic of 1837 involved interacting domestic and international pressures, including credit contraction, scarce specie and falling cotton prices. Van Buren inherited the crisis and suffered politically. The [archived presidential biography](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/about/presidents/martinvanburen) places the policy and panic in that sequence.

## Native sovereignty and forced removal

“Five Civilized Tribes” is a historical racialized label for Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek/Muscogee and Seminole peoples. It reflects White judgments about assimilation, not an objective ranking of civilizations. The nations had distinct governments, cultures and experiences; adoption of Anglo-American practices did not protect them from dispossession.

Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act on May 28, 1830. **Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831)** failed on the Supreme Court's original-jurisdiction question; the Court did not treat the Cherokee Nation as a foreign state for that purpose. **Worcester v. Georgia (1832)** was a different case: Marshall's Court invalidated the Georgia law at issue and recognized Cherokee territorial political rights. The [1831 opinion](https://www.loc.gov/item/usrep030001/) and [1832 opinion](https://www.loc.gov/item/usrep031515/) are available from the Library of Congress.

Legal resistance did not stop removal. The major forced Cherokee migration in 1838–1839 occurred under **Martin Van Buren**, carrying forward Jackson's policy. Thousands died; approximately 4,000 is a commonly cited estimate, not an exact count. Cherokee petitions, resistance and survival belong in the account alongside coercion and loss. See NPS [Preludes to the Trail of Tears](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/preludes-trail-of-tears.htm), its [transportation history](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/introduction-transportation-during-the-cherokee-removal-1837-1839.htm), and [contemporary witness material](https://www.nps.gov/fosm/learn/historyculture/storiestrailoftears.htm).

## Campaign images and majority power

The 1840 Whigs promoted Harrison and Tyler with military imagery, log cabins and hard cider, presenting Harrison as an ordinary man despite his wealthy origins. Campaign image and biography were different things. See the NPS [Election of 1840](https://home.nps.gov/mava/learn/historyculture/the-election-of-1840.htm). Tocqueville's warning about tyranny of the majority asks whether majority opinion can silence minorities and individuals; expanded participation alone did not guarantee their rights.

## Qualifications to the supplied text

- **Turnout dates:** the edited text attributes roughly one-quarter turnout to 1828. The [US Census historical series Y27–78](https://www2.census.gov/prod2/statcomp/documents/CT1970p2-12.pdf) reports 26.9% for 1824, 57.6% for 1828 and 80.2% for 1840. The quiz asks about increased participation without requiring the conflicting printed figure.
- **The “booze” origin story:** the word was in use before the 1840 campaign. The [1811 primary dictionary](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5402/pg5402.html) already contains its drink-related forms. The game does not teach the supplied company's origin story.
- **Office turnover:** the text's approximately 50% figure has no defined denominator or period. Questions test rotation, loyalty and corruption rather than an uncertain percentage.
- **Claims of a first:** the game teaches growing popular choice of electors and mass campaigning. It does not claim that no earlier election had popular participation or that “first modern election” is a settled literal first.
